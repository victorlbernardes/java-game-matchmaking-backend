# ADR-0021 — SLOs, error budget, and the burn-rate alerts (W16)

## Status
Accepted (2026-10-18)

## Context
ADR 0020 made the queue observable: the engine and the API expose KPIs, Prometheus scrapes both,
Grafana draws them. W15 ended with a number that goes up and a dashboard nobody is obliged to
look at. A dashboard answers *what is happening*; it does not answer **"is this good enough, and
who has to act?"** — and it cannot be an objective, because an objective needs a target, a
window and a consequence.

W16 was the load sprint. Measuring the ceiling with the load generator (`requests/load/`) is what
turns "the KPIs are exposed" into "here is the line, and here is the number of players per second
at which the line still holds". The same load run also found three defects (this ADR's
Consequences) — and the most useful thing about the objectives written here is that two of the
three would have *paged* instead of being found by hand:

| Defect found by the W16 load (before the fix) | Which objective would have caught it |
| --- | --- |
| `pair.lua` read the queue head itself while the caller stored a roster read earlier: overlapping ticks removed the *wrong* members (players in two matches, others off the queue with nothing to pair) — 40 matches refused `tickets not in CONFIRMING`, 40 dissolved, 186 tickets off the queue unpaired | SL-2 (confirm ratio) fires on the dissolved matches; SL-4 (queue head) fires on the 186 players left waiting with nothing pairing from the queue |
| The confirmation path published the event per confirmation: 312 `MATCH_CONFIRMED` rows for 204 matches (the match insert is an upsert, so the duplicates survived) | SL-2 measured it as formed > confirmed and nothing else; the duplicate itself is a Postgres invariant the load test asserts. This is the objective's blind spot, and it is why the load test counts rows instead of trusting the ratio |
| A single-page `SCAN` in the pairing path stopped seeing the queue once it had 404 keys (the queue sat on page 6 of 39): the engine paired once and then went completely blind | SL-4 — a queue with waiting players whose head waits minutes |

## Options

**A. Alerts on raw KPIs.** `matchmaking_queue_oldest_wait_seconds > 30`. Cheap, no rules file.
Rejected: every threshold is re-derived in the alert, there is no notion of a window, and a
one-minute spike pages exactly like a sustained degradation. It also cannot express a *budget*:
"we may be slow for 1% of matches this month" has nowhere to live.

**B. SLOs as ratios + burn-rate alerts (the Google SRE workbook shape).** The objective is a
ratio over a window (`within/total`), the error budget is `1 - target`, and the alerts fire on the
*burn rate* — how fast the budget is being spent — in two windows (fast: 14.4× over 1h+5m → page;
slow: 6× over 6h+30m → ticket). Chosen.

**C. Only thresholds, no ratios ("threshold SLOs").** What a first pass looks like: every
objective is a gauge compared to a line. Rejected as the *whole* set because the two objectives
that matter most (latency, confirmation) are naturally proportions — "99% of matched tickets" —
and a threshold on a gauge cannot express "of the last N", so it cannot distinguish a busy minute
from a broken matchmaker. Adopted **within** the set for the two objectives that have no
denominator: SL-3 (delivery) and SL-4 (capacity) are lines, and the ADR says why below.

**D. Push-based percentile aggregation (Micrometer's own percentiles).** Rejected: this stack
publishes to Prometheus, which computes `histogram_quantile` from buckets. Micrometer's
aggregated percentiles would be a second, differently-windowed answer to the same question.

## Decision

Four objectives, a 1% error budget on the two ratios, and burn-rate alerts that are quiet by
default. The rules live in the repository (`ops/prometheus/rules/matchmaking-slos.yml`, 10
recording rules + 8 alerts) so an objective is reviewed like code, and Prometheus refuses to
start on a broken rule file — an objective that does not compile is worse than none.

| Id | Objective | Metric | Shape | Why this line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SL-1 | 99% of matched tickets wait ≤ 10 s | `matchmaking_match_wait_seconds_bucket{le=10}` / `..._count` | ratio, budget 1% | the number a player feels; 10 s is where the confirmation window (30 s) is still comfortable |
| SL-2 | 99% of formed matches are confirmed | `matches_confirmed_total` / `matches_formed_total` | ratio, budget 1% | a formed match that dissolves is a player who was told "match found" and then lost it |
| SL-3 | the outbox never ages past 5 s, and parks nothing | `outbox_oldest_pending_seconds`, `outbox_backlog{status="PARKED"}` | threshold | the relay runs on a 250 ms timer (ADR 0018); seconds mean trouble, `PARKED` means a human |
| SL-4 | a waiting player never waits > 30 s while his queue has work | `queue_oldest_wait_seconds`, `queue_depth` | threshold (head + depth in the same mode/region) | this is "the matchmaker is alive", not "it is fast" |

Why SL-3 and SL-4 are not ratios: there is no proportion to spend. "The oldest pending event is
under 5 s" is a line, and a backlog either crosses it or does not. Forcing them into a ratio would
mean inventing a denominator (events under 5 s / all events) whose failures are exactly the
interesting ones — and 1% of events late is not a budget anyone wants to spend. They alert after
the line has been held for a while (`for: 2m` for a stale backlog, `1m` for a stuck queue).

Supporting alert: `MatchmakingServiceDown` on `up{job=~"matchmaking-.*"} == 0`. A service that is
not scraped stops producing every ratio above, and "no data" must never be read as "no burn".

## How it works

1. **Recording rules** compute the ratios (`matchmaking:slo_latency_within_10s:ratio_{5m,30m,1h,6h}`,
   `matchmaking:slo_confirm:ratio_*`) every 15 s, so dashboards and alerts read the same series and
   cannot disagree about the objective.
2. **Burn-rate alerts** fire on the budget, not on the value: fast burn = 2% of the 30-day budget
   in one hour (14.4×, both the 1h and the 5m window burning → page); slow burn = 10% in six hours
   (6×, 6h and 30m → ticket). Two windows is what separates a page from a spike.
3. **The queue rules are derived from the queue itself** (`matchmaking:queue:waiting_players`,
   `matchmaking:queue:oldest_wait_seconds` are sums of the gauges ADR 0020 derives from Redis), so
   SL-4 cannot drift away from the thing it measures.
4. **The SL-1 boundary is a bucket edge.** `EngineKpis.LATENCY_SLO = 10 s` is passed to the Timer's
   `serviceLevelObjectives`, because the objective is only measurable at a boundary the histogram
   actually has (see the finding below).
5. **The wiring is in the repository**: `ops/prometheus/prometheus.yml` (container) and
   `prometheus.host.yml` (binaries on the host) both list `rule_files`, and `docker-compose.yml`
   mounts `ops/prometheus/rules`. The host file's path is relative to the config file's directory,
   which is not where the repository root is.

## Measured numbers

The load generator drives real players through the whole stack (auth → API → engine →
Redis/Postgres/Kafka) with an event-driven auto-confirmer standing in for the players' clients
(`requests/mm.sh load`, JSON per run in `requests/load/results-*.json`). All runs: 1v1/NA,
every player pairable, one confirmation per player.

| Run | Players | Enqueue | Pairing (peak / window) | Formed → confirmed | Dissolved | Duplicate events | Wait p50 / p95 / p99 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **before** (pre-W16 loop: one match per tick, and the pool + `SCAN` defects) | 400 | 12/400 enqueued (388 refused: `pool reached max wait queue size of 24`) | — / 4.2 players/s | 200 → 302 | 2 | yes (asymmetry below) | 30 / 30 / 30 s |
| after, 400 | 400 | 265/s | 89 / 62 players/s | 200 → 200 | 0 | 0 | 1.23 / 1.74 / 1.78 s |
| after, 2000 | 2000 | 282/s | 168 / 125 players/s | 1000 → 1000 | 0 | 0 | 6.16 / 9.22 / 9.86 s |
| after, 4000 (arrival above capacity) | 4000 | 269/s | 184 / 138 players/s | 2000 → 2000 | 0 | 0 | 9.41 / 14.97 / 15.59 s |

Read as: the ceiling went from **4.2 to 184 players/s peak** (≈44×) for the same 400-player load,
and the objectives hold up to ≈180 players/s of pairing on one node — above that the queue grows,
waits grow with it, and SL-4 (not SL-1) is the objective that reports it. That is the honest
reading of the 4000-player row: 4000 players arriving at 269/s into a node that pairs 184/s cannot
all wait under 10 s, and no objective should pretend otherwise. SL-1 promises latency *up to
capacity*; SL-4 promises that nobody is abandoned past 30 s, which is the property that must hold
at any arrival rate.

Cost at the ceiling: engine CPU 0.14–0.19 cores, heap peak 77 MB, Redis ~2–3.4k ops/s, Postgres
~124–151 tx/s. The engine is not CPU-bound at 184 players/s; the pairing loop's per-match
round trips are the limit, which is what the batch parameter (`PAIR_BATCH_PER_TICK = 200`) buys.

## How to test

```bash
# the whole scenario, hand-runnable, 32 checks (rules loaded, ratios agree with a hand count,
# the queue rules follow the queue, the alerts are quiet and alive, the budget is unspent)
bash requests/observability/check_slos.sh

# the rules alone, without a stack (what CI can always run)
promtool check rules ops/prometheus/rules/matchmaking-slos.yml
```

The scenario's negative control is the interesting part: an alert expression that never answers
anything never fires either, so it evaluates each expression at a threshold that *must* trip it
(`(1 - ratio) >= 0`, `oldest_wait > -1`) and requires a series, then at the real threshold and
requires none. That is the check that would have failed on the two silent-defect variants found
while writing this ADR (below).

The load test is the other half of the DoD:

```bash
requests/mm.sh load --players 400 --concurrency 64 --label after
```

(The harness was Python when this ADR was written; ADR 0040 moved it to
`tools/scenarios`, and the command above is the same measurement.)

It exits non-zero when a match produced more than one event (the invariant that a ratio cannot
see), and writes `requests/load/results-<label>.json`.

## Findings (found while measuring, all fixed in W16)

1. **A declared objective that measures nothing.** The SL-1 rule was written as
   `..._bucket{le="10"}`. Micrometer's default percentile buckets jump from 8.59 s to 10.02 s, so
   **no bucket had `le="10"`**: `sum(rate(...))` matched nothing, the ratio answered `NaN`/empty,
   and the objective silently measured no data. Fixed by passing the objective's boundary to the
   Timer (`serviceLevelObjectives(Duration.ofSeconds(10))`). Measured with the running engine:
   `count(matchmaking_match_wait_seconds_bucket{le="10"})` → **0 series** before, **1** after.
2. **A bucket edge that does not match its own rule.** Even with the edge present, Micrometer
   exposes it as `le="10.0"` while PromQL label matching is exact-string, so `le="10"` still
   matched nothing. Fixed by matching either rendering (`le=~"10(\\.0)?"`) and by keeping the
   scenario's "the recorded ratio is a number" check, which fails loudly instead of reporting 0.
3. **A rule file that loads nothing looks healthy.** `rule_files: [ops/prometheus/rules/*.yml]` in
   the host config resolved to `ops/prometheus/ops/prometheus/rules/*.yml` (Prometheus resolves
   relative entries against the config file's directory), loaded **zero groups**, and reported no
   error. `promtool check config` was happy; only asking `/api/v1/rules` for the groups showed it.
   Fixed (`rules/*.yml`) and guarded: the scenario asserts the groups exist *and* that their
   `file` is this repository's.
4. **The load generator itself was the loss.** With a `docker exec redis-cli SUBSCRIBE` client,
   24 of 1024 matches were announced but never reached the client, and each dissolved at its 30 s
   deadline (SL-2 = 97.7%). The engine had published them: the exec-attach stream in front of the
   client was the lossy hop. The generator now speaks RESP over a socket (like a real client does
   over a WS) and the same 2000/4000-player runs dissolve **zero** matches. A measurement harness
   that drops 2% of its own input cannot measure a 99% objective.

## Trade-offs

- **A 1% budget is a promise, not a measurement.** It is calibrated at the capacity measured above;
  the ADR files that as an *envelope*, not as a guarantee about any arrival rate.
- **SL-2's ratio cannot see duplicate events.** Two events for one match keep
  `confirmed/formed == 1.0`. The invariant lives in the load test's Postgres count (this is a real
  blind spot of ratio-based objectives, and it is why the DoD has both).
- **Threshold objectives (SL-3/SL-4) can flap** with a single unlucky sample. Mitigated with
  `for:` durations, which costs detection time.
- **Ratios need cardinality discipline.** Every new mode/region multiplies the series; the rules
  aggregate with `sum()` and never group by player, so cardinality stays a function of the modes.
- **The alert set is deliberately small.** Four objectives and one supporting alert; a rule that
  nobody would act on is a page that trains people to ignore pages.

## Consequences

- An objective is now a reviewable artifact: changing SL-1's boundary means changing
  `EngineKpis.LATENCY_SLO`, the rule file and this table together — the scenario fails if they drift
  apart (the ratio stops being a number).
- The W15 dashboard and the W16 alerts read the same recorded series, so the picture and the
  promise cannot disagree.
- The two real defects the load found were *not* caught by an alert, because they were never
  released: they were found in the load run, before the numbers were recorded. The objectives are
  the reason the next one is paged instead.
- `requests/load/` is now part of the Definition of Done ("there is a measured number"): every
  sprint that claims a throughput or latency change re-runs it and commits
  `results-<label>.json`.
- Known open item (recorded in `MelhoriasFutura.md`): the notification path is fire-and-forget
  pub/sub with no re-presentation, so a client that misses the frame learns about the match only
  by its own resync. The contract (`03`) promises the heartbeat re-presents it; nothing implements
  that yet, and a future sprint owns it. SL-2 is the objective that will show it when a real
  client is the lossy part.
